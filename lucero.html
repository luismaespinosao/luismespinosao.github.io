Lucero

Therapeutic Device for Child Support During Parental Separation

A transitional therapeutic object — an astronaut-suited stuffed animal — designed with and for children aged 5–10 navigating parental separation. Lucero monitors the child's grieving process through voice interaction and provides emotional support through storytelling, self-esteem reinforcement, and daily care reminders.

01 — Context

The Problem

In México, 166,766 divorces were registered in 2022 — an 11.4% increase from the previous year. Of these, 48.1% involved minor children. In Oaxaca alone, 2,599 divorces were recorded that same year.

Children of separated parents face elevated risks of behavioral disorders, poor academic performance, anxiety, loneliness, and long-term psychiatric conditions. Yet in Huajuapan de León, 26,321 people live in moderate poverty — making private psychological support inaccessible to most families.

Public institutions like DIF provide limited capacity for specialized follow-up. The question became: could a transitional object — something a child could hold, talk to, and feel accompanied by — serve as a therapeutic bridge?

02 — Methodology

Design Process

The project combined Design Thinking and Co-Design, actively involving children in every phase of development — not as subjects to be studied, but as co-creators of their own therapeutic tool.

Semi-Structured Interviews Expert Consultation Co-Design with Children Storyboard Lo-Fi Prototyping Physical Prototyping Wizard of Oz Think-Aloud Protocol Likert Scale Evaluation
01 — Empathize

Expert Interviews

Conducted 8 semi-structured interviews with child and school psychologists across Oaxaca. They identified key emotional challenges: guilt over parental absence, low self-esteem, poor academic performance, sleep and feeding problems, social isolation, and aggression.

All experts validated that transitional objects — combined with play therapy, art therapy, and music therapy — are effective therapeutic tools for this population.

02 — Define + Ideate

Co-Design with Children at DIF

Co-design sessions with children aged 5–10 at DIF municipal facilities. Children drew their ideal object shapes, selected iconography for the device's functions (star = voice diary, book with face = story, checkbox = reminders), and customized a stuffed animal prototype to reflect their personal preferences.

Their strong attachment to plush toys guided the final design direction — an astronaut-suited stuffed animal with tactile buttons.

03 — Prototype

From Sketch to Physical Prototype

Started with a storyboard lo-fi prototype, then built a medium-fidelity physical prototype: a base stuffed animal modified with an astronaut suit, embossed buttons with the children's chosen iconography, an integrated speaker for voice interaction, and colored LED lights.

First iteration included red/blue LEDs — feedback prompted a switch to warmer tones in V2.0.

04 — Validate

2-Phase Usability Testing

Phase 1 (DIF facility): 9 children, 5 tasks, Wizard of Oz + Think-Aloud Protocol. 64% task completion, 94% satisfaction. Children named the device "Lucero" — 90% acceptance of the name.

Phase 2 (UsaLab, UTM): 6 children, 6 tasks using V2.0. 90% task completion, 97% satisfaction. Children showed stronger emotional attachment — some asked to keep Lucero.

03 — Findings

Key Design Decisions

Transitional Object

Children's strong preference for plush toys over electronic devices shaped the entire product direction — a physical companion they could hold, not a screen they would use.

Child-Led Iconography

The interface icons were selected by the children themselves during co-design, ensuring intuitive understanding without reading ability. 46% had iconography issues in V1 — resolved in V2.

Emotional Bonding

Children showed genuine affective attachment by Phase 2 — hugging Lucero spontaneously, responding emotionally to phrases like "Yo también te quiero mucho," and asking to take Lucero home.

"Some children asked to keep Lucero at the end of the session, and expressed their affection when Lucero told them emotional phrases."
